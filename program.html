14:00-14:30Haim Avron, Tel Aviv University
Lean for Applied Mathematics – a personal experience
Abstract

Lean is a proof assistant: mathematical statements and proofs are written as code, and compiling the code verifies every step. One can argue that it is desirable for every paper to be accompanied by Lean code, so that others can be convinced of its correctness without verifying the proofs themselves. However, until recently, this was a utopian vision: formalization was far too laborious for everyday research, and for important theorems it was a research project in its own right. AI is changing this. It enables a new division of labor, one that is realistic: AI writes the Lean code, Lean checks the proofs, and the human checks the statements.

In this talk, I will share my personal experience trying to make Lean part of my daily research workflow in numerical linear algebra. I will discuss formalizing one of my papers. The formalization uncovered problems in the paper, including typos and missing assumptions that had survived multiple revisions. I will also discuss my experience with formalizing lecture notes, formalizing a paper as it is being written, and using formalization as a tool for reviewing papers.

I will discuss possible pitfalls as well: Lean formalization should not be used in a "formalize and forget" manner. For example, in a few places the AI substituted weaker statements, which I caught only by checking the Lean statements against the paper. I will describe what must be checked and my current workflow, and end with a proposal: papers in applied mathematics should come with Lean formalizations, and our community should build the libraries that make this routine.

No prior knowledge of Lean is assumed (and I am not a Lean expert either).

15:30-16:30Discussion, moderated by Arik Yochelis, Ben-Gurion University
AI & I — on doing and evaluating science in the age of AI
Abstract

If AI makes our work faster, cheaper and broader, is there a problem? I come to this question from two sides. As a user, I rely on AI to compute, code, draft and read beyond my field. As an associate editor of Nonlinearity, I watch the same tools reshape submissions and refereeing. This perspective aims to stimulate AI awareness: knowing what these tools can and cannot do, and how they may affect our sense of understanding and novelty. I hope to open a general discussion, with questions such as how authors, editors and referees should use AI, how we can recognize real novelty and maintain mathematical methodologies, and what AI means for education and scientific advances.
